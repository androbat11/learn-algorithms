How to use this

Each item says what mastery means — a thing you can do from a blank file, not a thing you have read about. That is the bar. If you cannot do it without looking, it is not done, however familiar it feels. Familiarity is the illusion; the blank file is the test.

Tier 0 · Foundations

non-negotiable

Not topics so much as equipment. Everything after this assumes them, and skipping them is why people grind hundreds of problems without improving.

  • The problem contract done · lesson 1 Given, Goal, Bounds, Trace, Breaks — in under three minutes on an unseen problem, before writing code.
  • Big-O complexity ready · lesson 5 Read time and space complexity straight off the shape of the code. Nested loop → n². Halving → log n. Recursion that splits → n log n. Convert an input bound into a verdict about which approach is allowed.
  • Arrays & strings Know the cost of every built-in you reach for. includes and indexOf are O(n); shift and unshift are O(n); slice copies. Accidental O(n²) from a linear scan inside a loop is the single most common self-inflicted wound.
  • Recursion ready · lesson 4 Write a base case that always terminates. Trust a recursive call before the function is finished. Trace the call tree by hand. Know why a missing base case overflows the stack rather than returning something wrong.

Tier 1 · The core

~80% of questions

If you master only one tier, master this one. These structures and patterns cover the large majority of what interviews actually ask, and every one of them is reachable in a 20-minute session.

  • Hash maps & sets ready · lesson 3 Complement lookup, frequency counting, grouping by a computed key. The tell: an inner loop searching for a value you can compute. Map when you need where, Set when you need whether.
  • Two pointers Opposite ends closing inward (sorted arrays, palindromes, container problems) and fast/slow in the same direction (cycle detection, finding a midpoint). The tell: a sorted array, or a question about pairs.
  • Sliding window Fixed size and variable size. Expand right, contract left while a condition is violated. The tell: "longest / shortest / maximum contiguous subarray or substring such that…".
  • Binary search you have this On a sorted array, and — the part most people miss — on an answer space. Write it without an off-by-one. Know which half to discard and why lo + (hi - lo) / 2 exists.
  • Sorting ready · lessons 2, 4 Insertion sort and merge sort from scratch. Stable vs unstable, adaptive vs not, in-place vs not. Know when to stop showing off and just call .sort((a,b) => a-b).
  • Stack Matching pairs (brackets), undo semantics, and the monotonic stack for "next greater element". The tell: you need the most recent unresolved thing.
  • Queue FIFO, and the engine that makes BFS work. Know why arr.shift() is O(n) in JavaScript and what to do instead when it matters.
  • Linked lists on your list Singly and doubly. Insert, delete, reverse, detect a cycle, find the middle. The skill underneath: pointer manipulation without losing the rest of the list — always save next before you overwrite it.
  • Binary trees on your list Build one. All four traversals: in-order, pre-order, post-order, level-order. Depth and height. Recursive first, then iterative with an explicit stack.
  • Binary search trees Insert, search, delete, validate. Know that in-order traversal of a BST is sorted — that single fact solves a surprising number of problems.
  • Heaps / priority queues What the heap property is, and top-k problems. JavaScript has no built-in heap, so know the standard implementation over an array, or a sort-based fallback and its cost.

Tier 2 · Expected

most loops reach here

Beyond the core but still routinely asked. Each introduces a genuinely new way of thinking rather than a new data structure, which is why they take longer than their length suggests.

  • Graphs Adjacency list vs matrix. DFS and BFS, and knowing which to reach for — BFS for shortest path in an unweighted graph, DFS for connectivity and exploration. Grid problems are graph problems in disguise.
  • Backtracking Subsets, permutations, combinations. The choose / explore / un-choose skeleton. The tell: "find all…" combined with a small input bound.
  • Intervals Merge overlapping, insert into a sorted set, detect conflicts. Almost always begins with sorting by start time — the whole family is one insight repeated.
  • 1-D dynamic programming Recognise overlapping subproblems. Recursion → memoisation → tabulation, in that order. Climbing stairs, house robber, coin change. The tell: "how many ways" or "min/max over choices".

Tier 3 · Beyond the budget

skip unless the loop demands it

Real topics, genuinely out of reach in 20–28 hours alongside everything above. Listed so you know what you are consciously deferring rather than what you have accidentally missed. Revisit after December, or if a specific company's loop is known to go here.

  • 2-D dynamic programming Grid paths, edit distance, longest common subsequence.
  • Tries Prefix trees. Narrow but decisive when autocomplete or word-search comes up.
  • Advanced graphs Topological sort, Dijkstra, union-find. Union-find is the highest-value of the three.
  • Greedy Locally optimal choices, and — the hard part — proving one actually works.
  • Bit manipulation XOR tricks, masks, counting set bits. Rarely decisive, occasionally the whole question.

If everything slips

Time will get compressed — it always does. In priority order, the things that would still leave you employable:

  1. Complexity + the contract. Without these you cannot even discuss a solution, and interviewers read that as not knowing.
  2. Hash maps, two pointers, sliding window. Three patterns, an enormous share of easy and medium array questions.
  3. Binary trees and traversals. The most reliably asked data structure in the entire field.
  4. Binary search. Cheap to learn, frequently asked, and embarrassing to fumble.
  5. Linked lists. Less common than they used to be, but they are on your list and they teach pointer discipline nothing else does.

How to practise, not just study

Lessons build knowledge. Problems build the skill, and the two are not interchangeable. A workable split for a 20-minute session:

New topic
One lesson. Learn the shape, write the reference implementation once from a blank file.
Next day
One problem in that topic. Contract first, always. Twenty minutes hard stop — then read the solution if stuck, and write down why you were stuck.
Day after
A second problem in the same topic, so the pattern is retrieved rather than recognised.
Weekly
Re-solve one problem from two weeks ago, cold. This is the spacing, and it is the part everyone skips.
The trap this plan exists to avoid

Solving many problems in one topic then never returning feels productive and builds almost nothing durable. Retrieval after partial forgetting is what creates lasting memory — which means the uncomfortable session, where you have half-forgotten and have to reconstruct, is the one that counts. Comfortable practice is mostly wasted practice.
