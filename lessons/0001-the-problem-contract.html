Lesson 01 · Problem solving

The Problem Contract

You do not freeze because you lack knowledge. You freeze because you are trying to answer an unanswerable question. Here is how to replace it with five answerable ones.

≈20 minutes · TypeScript · Companion card: The Problem Contract

There is a specific moment you have described, and it is worth being precise about it. The problem appears. You read it twice. You understand every word in it. And then your hands are on the keyboard and nothing happens — not because you do not know what a hash map is, but because the only question in your head is how do I solve this?

That question has no handle on it. It is too big to pick up. Your mind searches for the whole answer at once, fails to find it, and reports back as blankness. This is not a knowledge failure and grinding more problems will not fix it, because more problems just means more repetitions of the same unanswerable question.The pattern-based prep industry — Grokking, NeetCode, AlgoExpert — exists because of exactly this. Their shared claim is that a small number of recurring problem shapes cover most interview questions, so the useful skill is classification, not invention. We get to classification in Lesson 2. First we need something to classify.

The fix is old. In 1945 the Hungarian mathematician George Pólya wrote How to Solve It, and split problem solving into four phases: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, and look back. Every coding-interview framework you will ever be sold is a repackaging of those four (Pólya's four phases). The important part is not the list. It is that Pólya put understand in its own phase, with its own guiding questions, and insisted you finish it before planning anything.

Freezing is what happens when you skip straight to phase three.

This lesson drills phase one and nothing else. One tool, one habit, one win.

Before we start · from memory

Think of the last algorithm problem that beat you. What did you actually do in the first minute after reading it? Be honest and specific — write two or three lines.

The common answer

Most people write some version of: "I re-read it, then I started typing a loop to see where it went." Re-reading feels like understanding, but it is recognition, not comprehension — and starting a loop is phase three. If what you wrote resembles this, that is the thing this lesson replaces. If it does not, tell your teacher what you wrote; it changes what you should be taught next.

The toolFive lines before any code

A problem contract is five lines you write — physically write, in a comment block or on paper — before your first line of real code. It takes about three minutes. It is not note-taking. It is the act of converting one unanswerable question into five answerable ones.

Given
Every input, with its type and shape. Not "an array" — "an array of number, may be empty, may contain duplicates."
Goal
The exact thing to return, as a type. Values or indices? One answer or all of them? What comes back when nothing matches?
Bounds
How big can it get, what range do the values span. This line quietly tells you which complexity you are allowed to have.
Trace
One small example, worked by hand, no code. If you cannot produce the output yourself, you do not yet understand the problem.
Breaks
The inputs that would embarrass you. Empty, single element, all identical, no valid answer.

Notice what each line has in common: you can answer it in isolation, without knowing the solution. That is the entire mechanism. "How do I solve this?" has no entry point. "Can the array be empty?" has an obvious one, and answering it moves you.

WorkedTwo Sum, contracted

Here is the problem as an interviewer would say it, out loud, deliberately underspecified — because that is how they actually say it:

The problem, as spoken

"Given an array of integers and a target, return the two numbers that add up to the target."

Three minutes with the contract:

// Given:  nums: number[]  — unsorted, may contain duplicates and negatives
//         target: number
// Goal:   number[] of length 2 — INDICES, not values (had to ask)
//         exactly one answer is guaranteed (had to ask)
// Bounds: 2 <= nums.length <= 10^4  →  O(n^2) is 10^8, too slow. Need O(n).
// Trace:  nums = [2, 7, 11, 15], target = 9
//         2 + 7 = 9  →  indices [0, 1]
// Breaks: two identical numbers summing to target, e.g. [3,3] target 6
//         negative numbers: [-1, -2] target -3
//         same element used twice — not allowed

Read what happened there. Two of those lines contain the words had to ask, and each one was a real ambiguity that would have cost you a rewrite. The Bounds line ruled out the nested loop before you typed it — not by cleverness, but by arithmetic. And the Breaks line surfaced [3,3], which is the single most common way a Two Sum solution fails in an interview.

You still have not solved anything. But you are no longer staring at a blank page: you are looking at a list of facts, and the next question — which structure gives me O(1) lookup by value? — is one you can already answer.You know the answer. That is the point being made here. The knowledge was never missing; the path to it was.

RetrievalNow close the page in your head

Do not scroll up. Retrieving this while it feels slightly out of reach is what makes it stick — recalling something you read a minute ago builds almost nothing, while recalling it at the edge of forgetting builds a lot.Robert Bjork's desirable difficulties: conditions that slow practice down — spacing, retrieval, interleaving — produce more durable learning, while fluent, easy practice creates a convincing illusion of mastery. From memory

Write the five lines of the problem contract, in order, and one phrase saying what each is for.

The five lines

Given — every input, with type and shape.
Goal — the exact return type; values or indices.
Bounds — sizes and ranges; tells you the complexity allowed.
Trace — one example worked by hand, no code.
Breaks — the inputs that would embarrass you.

Missed one? That is useful information, not a failure — the one you dropped is the one to watch for tomorrow. Most people drop Bounds, because it feels like trivia right up until it is the line that tells you your approach is too slow.

Today's repOne problem, no solution

This is the part that matters, and it should take ten minutes.

  1. Open a problem you have never seen

    Go to NeetCode's practice list and pick anything from Arrays & Hashing. Easy is fine. Easy is better, actually — the point is not difficulty.

  2. Write the contract. Do not solve it.

    Five lines in a comment block, in a .ts file. Three minutes. Then stop — genuinely stop, and do not write the solution. Sitting with a fully understood, unsolved problem is the uncomfortable part, and it is the part doing the work.

  3. Check the constraints against your contract

    Reveal the problem's stated constraints and compare them with your Bounds and Breaks lines. Every edge case you missed is a genuine finding about your own blind spots — write it down.

  4. Bring it back

    Paste the contract to your teacher. Feedback on the contract is the feedback loop for this skill; you cannot self-grade the questions you did not think to ask.

The trap

You will want to solve it. The pull is strong and it feels like productivity. But solving is phase three, and phase three is the one you can already do — you have shipped real software; carrying out a plan is not your problem. Practising the phase you are already good at is how three months disappear with nothing changed.
